Diagnosing concentration and cycling without knowing the preference

2026-10-19. Step 1 of the practical-diagnostics plan for Paper B (JMLR v4, last pushed as 46fd577). This report covers five tests that estimate the loop's two risks without the preference matrix P, and checks them against runs and data we already have. No new training and no VM time were used. Scripts and outputs are in reviews/2026-10-19-practical-diagnostics/analysis/, and every number is listed in text/sources.md.

Answer.Two numbers decide what iterated preference optimization does.
  • The effective refresh α′ sets how sharp the loop's target is. When α′ ≥ 1 there is no finite target at all.
  • The cyclic gain γB at the target decides whether a given refresh weight lets the loop settle or makes it rotate.
Both can be estimated without P.
  • α′ comes from the model's own log-probabilities on a fixed probe set in the first rounds (D1), or from re-judging the same pairs after one round (D5).
  • γB comes from a small judged sample (D3), or is flagged early from the trajectory (D2).
  • A permutation test (D4) shows whether pooling sources created cycles.
Validation, with no new runs.
  • D1, on 24 trained runs. It recovered α′ within 0.025 on 10 of 12 DPO runs. It flagged 3 of the 4 runs that have no finite target and none of the others.
  • D2, from rounds 1–12 only. With its threshold chosen on the other runs, it caught 91% of the panels still oscillating at the end, with 74% specificity.
  • D3, at 36 judge calls per prompt. A census of the cyclic gain found all three at-risk prompts out of 130, with 0.3% false alarms. The usual census of Condorcet winners reported no winner on 49% of prompts, against 3.1% in truth.
  • D4. It detected coupling in HelpSteer and stayed at its nominal level on independent criteria.
  • D5, in simulation. It recovered α′ within 0.01, with standard deviation at most 0.05, from 12,288 judge calls.
Uncertain.
  • D2's threshold and D1's flag level were set on these same runs.
  • D1 is coarse for IPO, with errors up to 0.23. It also misses hard-label DPO, which a simple label check catches.
  • D5 has only been tested against a judge whose bias we simulated. A real self-judging run (X13, about $7) would test it.

1The question and the two numbers

The loop. Iterated preference optimization trains each model on comparisons of its own answers, against a KL reference that is refreshed toward the latest model with weight α. The step size is β, the inverse of the DPO or IPO regularization. Paper B reduces the population version of this loop to an exact recursion, and shows two things:

  • Concentration. The loop aims at a target whose sharpness is B = β/(1 − α). Large B concentrates the policy, on a Condorcet winner when one exists.
  • Cycling. When the preference has a cyclic part, the target can be unstable and the loop rotates among answers. It settles locally iff α² + (1 − α)²γB² < 1. Here γB is the gain of the cyclic part at the target, set by the judge's preferences among the answers the target favours. Equivalently, for γB > 1 the refresh weight must exceed αloc = (γB² − 1)/(γB² + 1).

What practice lacks. A practitioner does not know P, and two things in real pipelines change the numbers the theory uses:

  • A judge that leans toward what the current model already likes adds hidden refresh. The manuscript's Corollary on judge bias and replay shows that a bias ε on the label log-odds turns α into α′ = α + βε.
  • An entropy bonus or popularity penalty η subtracts βη.
  • Pooled data from several criteria can contain cycles that no single criterion has (the manuscript's pooled-sources Theorem).

So the practical questions are three:

  • Is my α′ close to 1?
  • Is my target too sharp?
  • Is γB large enough that my α will not settle?

The two numbers.

  • Effective refresh α′. It gives the effective sharpness B′ = β/(1 − α′), which controls concentration. α′ ≥ 1 means no finite target.
  • Cyclic gain γB at the planned sharpness. It controls rotation, through αloc.

Figure 1 shows both on runs from the paper. In (a), a judge bias moves the end entropy exactly along the curve of α′. In (b), at the same B = 12, the balanced planted panels oscillate at α = 0.2 and 0.5 and settle at 0.8 and 0.9, on the two sides of αloc ≈ 0.59.

3Why the tests work

Each test rests on one short statement. None is a new theorem. Each is a consequence of results already in the manuscript, plus standard sampling arguments. Step 2 would add them as one corollary, two lemmas and two remarks.

A (Corollary; D1). Suppose the labels are additive under the link, so the cyclic part is C = 0, and the judge adds ε(xi − xj) of the current policy to each label's log-odds. Write for the change of the centred logits in round t. Then with α′ = α + βε, for every t. Hence, for any contrast v such as the log-odds of two answers, the ratio equals α′. When α′ < 1 the loop converges geometrically to the target of sharpness B′ = β/(1 − α′). When α′ ≥ 1 it has none.
Proof. By the judge-bias corollary of the manuscript, the recursion becomes with a constant c. Subtract the same identity at t − 1. ∎

When a round stops short of its optimum, the measured ratio is the round's actual contraction. This equals α′ only if each round is solved. The IPO errors in Section 5.1 are consistent with this, though we have not separated it from other causes.

B (Remark; D2). At a target x* that is a hyperbolic fixed point of the round map G, we have . The rotating eigenvalues of DG(x*) are α ± i(1 − α)γB, by the manuscript's local-spectrum Theorem. So the least-squares map M̂ fitted to consecutive increments tends to DG(x*) as the loop approaches the target. Its spectral radius ρ̂ then tends to , the quantity whose crossing of 1 is the stability edge. Complex eigenvalues mean rotation.

Rounds 1–12 are still far from the target, so ρ̂ is only a proxy there and its threshold has to be calibrated. The calibrated value, 0.83, lies below the theoretical 1.

C (Lemma; D3). Let come from n independent judgments of each ordered pair of a panel with K answers.
  • (i) Consistency. If the target of P is unique and continuous in P, then almost surely as n → ∞. Uniqueness and continuity hold under the conditions of the KL-contraction Proposition.
  • (ii) Noise inflates the cyclic part. The cyclic part of satisfies .
  • (iii) Winner censuses are unstable near ties. A pair with margin is misdirected by the majority of its 2n judgments with probability up to . One misdirected pair can remove the Condorcet winner.
Proof.
  • (i) Several maps are continuous: the Hodge projection (it is linear), the softmax Jacobian J, the target (by assumption), and the eigenvalues. The strong law gives .
  • (ii) The projection ΠC onto cyclic edge flows is orthogonal, with trace (K − 1)(K − 2)/2. Its diagonal is the same on every edge of the complete graph, by symmetry, so each entry is that trace divided by the K(K − 1)/2 edges, which is (K − 2)/K. Independent errors therefore add to the expected energy.
  • (iii) Hoeffding's inequality. ∎

Part (ii) gives the noise correction used in D3: subtract the estimated variance term before computing γ̂B. Part (iii) is why a census of Condorcet winners counts sampling noise as intransitivity. γB is continuous in P, but the existence of a winner is not.

D (Lemma; D4). Fix a prompt's labelled pairs and their source tags . Let a source's label on a pair depend only on the source and the pair. If the tags are exchangeable given the pairs, the observed labelled graph has the same law as the graph after a uniform random permutation of the tags. This holds in particular if each pair's source is drawn independently of the pair. Then the expected difference between the observed and permuted cycle rates is zero, and the permutation p-value is exact. When sources choose their own pairs, the difference measures the cycles that this coupling creates. This is the realized counterpart of the covariance term in the pooled-sources Theorem.
Proof. The standard randomization argument: under exchangeability, the observed assignment is a uniform draw from its permutation orbit. ∎
E (Remark; D5). Suppose the judge's log-odds for a pair at round r is a + εxr, where xr = sr(i) − sr(j) is the policy's log-ratio and a is fixed. Given the pair's total wins S over rounds 0 and 1, the round-1 wins follow Fisher's noncentral hypergeometric law with odds ratio exp(ε(x1 − x0)), which does not involve a.
  • Conditional fit. Maximizing this conditional likelihood gives an ε̂ that is consistent as the number of probe pairs grows, for any fixed n judgments per pair and round.
  • Fixed-effects fit. A logistic fit with one intercept per pair is biased by O(1/n), the incidental-parameter problem.
  • Frozen judge. Its labels do not move with xr, so ε̂ → 0. Correctly so: by the judge-bias corollary, its bias adds no refresh.

How accurate.

  • DPO, 12 runs with a numerical α′. The error is within 0.025 on 10 and within 0.05 on 11. The exception is the frozen judge, at +0.099, which we cannot yet explain.
  • IPO, 9 runs. The estimate is coarser: within 0.1 on 7. It is too high when α = 0 with β = 8 (+0.234) and when α = 0.5 with β = 1 (+0.162), and too low at α = 0.9 (−0.093).

Two biases, not yet separated.

  • Upward. A round that stops short of its optimum contracts by less than α′.
  • Downward. Slow loops with small steps leave few increments above the floor.

The collapse flag.

  • No run with a finite target exceeds 0.946.
  • Three of the four runs with no finite target exceed 0.98: full refresh 0.989, judge bias ε = 0.55 at 1.031, per-round KL control 1.197.
  • Hard-label DPO reads 0.907 and is missed. Its log-odds grow without bound but ever more slowly, so its increments look like a slow geometric decay.

A drift check failed to catch it. We tried comparing the increment ratio over rounds 1–5 with that over rounds 6–15. It rises for hard-label DPO (0.929 → 0.979), but just as much in runs with a finite target (E1 ε = 0.3: 0.799 → 0.892), so it does not separate them. The label check L catches this case without any trajectory: a pair whose labels are unanimous has no finite DPO optimum (the manuscript's result on deterministic labels).

  1. judge each ordered pair of its four answers n times;
  2. estimate P, and subtract the noise term of Statement C(ii) from its cyclic part;

The winner census fails. The judge has many near-ties. Even with 1,200 calls per prompt, a hard-majority census reports no Condorcet winner on 31% of prompts against 3.1% in truth, and can certify a winner on only half of them. This matters beyond our tests. Counting prompts without a Condorcet winner in sampled labels, the natural first check, will report widespread intransitivity in almost any noisy dataset.

The gain census works. At 36 calls per prompt it finds every at-risk prompt with 0.3% false alarms. Its prescription, α ≥ 0.72 at B = 10, is close to the true 0.70. The noise correction matters little here: the mean cyclic energy at 36 calls is 0.1295 raw and 0.1232 corrected, against 0.1176 in truth.

5.4 D4: pooled HelpSteer labels cycle because of how the pairs were built

Method. In the construction tested here, each criterion contributes the pair formed by its best and its worst answer. D4 compares the share of prompts whose labels contain a directed cycle with the share after the criteria are randomly reassigned among the same pairs (Statement D).

  • Controls. When three criteria each favour a different answer, coupling creates cycles: 94.3% against 47.3% with little noise, and 13.5% against 7.9% with more. With independent criteria the difference is 0.09 pp [−0.37, 0.50], so the test holds its level.
  • HelpSteer. Adding concision or simplicity to the quality criteria raises the observed rate above the permuted one, by 0.92 pp [0.65, 1.17] and 1.60 pp [1.24, 1.97]. Concision is negatively correlated with the quality criteria. On the three quality criteria alone the effect is tiny: 0.066 pp.
  • UltraFeedback. Its four aspects agree with one another, and the effect is 0.12 pp [0.002, 0.25].

The rates stay small (at most 3% of prompts), consistent with the prevalence results already in the paper.

7What a practitioner should do

Iterated training can go wrong in two ways:

  • the model ends up giving essentially one answer;
  • it keeps switching between a few answers and never settles.

Two settings matter:

  • Refresh weight α: how far the KL reference moves toward the current model each round. 0 keeps the initial model forever; 1 replaces it with the latest model every round.
  • Step size β: how far each round moves.

Together they set how sharp the end point is, B = β/(1 − α).

1

Before training: check the labels and how strongly the judge's preferences go in circles.

  • One-sided labels. If you use DPO, look for pairs whose labels are one-sided, where the judge always picks the same answer. On such a pair DPO keeps widening the gap however you set α and β. Use soft labels (probabilities), label smoothing, or IPO (check L).
  • Circular preferences. Take 50–100 prompts. Let the model write 4 answers to each, and have the judge compare every pair 3 times in each order, which is 36 calls per prompt. From these, compute for each prompt how strongly the preferences go in circles at your planned sharpness (γ̂B, D3).
    • If no prompt exceeds 1, the loop will not rotate. Only the sharpness needs watching.
    • If some do, raise α above the level the census gives (the largest αloc), and set β = (1 − α)B so the end point stays the same.
    • Do not count prompts that "lack a Condorcet winner": noisy judgments make about half of all prompts look that way.
  • Several criteria pooled. If your data pool several criteria (helpfulness, concision, …), shuffle which criterion labelled which pair and compare how often the labels form cycles (D4). If the real data cycle more, the way the pairs were built created the cycles. Then use the average verdict of all criteria for each pair, or tell the model which criterion applies. Do not let each criterion pick its own best-versus-worst pair.
2

In the first 10–15 rounds: watch a fixed probe set.

Keep 64 prompts with 4 fixed answers each. After every round, record the model's log-probability of each answer. This is one forward pass per answer, with no training. Read two things from it.

  • Will it stop? Track the gap between the two leading answers. Its change shrinks by a fixed factor each round, and that factor is the effective refresh α′ (D1).
    • Well below 1, for example 0.8: the loop will settle.
    • Near or above 1 (0.98 or more): it will keep sharpening and never stop.
    • β/(1 − α′) is the sharpness you will actually get. If that is more than you want, the end point is too sharp.
  • Will it rotate? Fit how the changes evolve from round to round over rounds 1–12. If the update direction turns around (a complex eigenvalue pair) without dying out (spectral radius 0.83 or more), the loop will very likely keep switching (D2).

If the judge is the model itself or shares its weights, judge the probe pairs once at the start and once after round 1. See whether the judge's preferences moved with the model's (D5). If they did, the judge is secretly refreshing the reference: your α becomes α + β × bias.

  • About 12,000 calls suffice.
  • Use the conditional-likelihood estimate; a plain regression misses most of the bias.
3

When an alarm fires: change the refresh, not the step controller.

  • Too sharp, or never stops.
    • Lower the sharpness B, through a smaller β or a smaller α.
    • Freeze the judge at the initial model, or subtract the measured bias.
    • Or add a penalty on popular answers.
    • Do not rely on a per-round KL controller: it keeps every round's step the same size, so the loop keeps going, and in our test the entropy fell to 6 × 10⁻⁶ (E3).
  • Rotation.
    • Raise α while keeping B fixed, with β = (1 − α)B.
    • Without any estimate, use the adaptive rule: every 10 rounds, if the size of the updates has not fallen, move α 30% of the way to 1 (E5).
    • Mixing in samples from earlier models helps only when α is low; at high α it causes rotation (E4).
  • Deploying a rotating loop. If it is already rotating and you want its average behaviour, serve a randomly chosen one of the last 30 checkpoints for each request. Averaging their weights does not give that average (E6).

Caveats:

  • The levels 0.98 and 0.83 were set on our runs.
  • The α′ reading is coarse for IPO.
  • The judge-bias check has been tested only in simulation so far.

X13 (needs your OK; about 1 VM hour, about $7). A self-judging loop, to test D5 and the hidden-refresh prediction with a real judge.

  • Setup. The current Qwen2.5-0.5B policy itself judges each pair in both orders. Soft-label DPO at α = 0.5, β = 1, 64 panels, 40 rounds. A second arm uses a frozen copy of the initial judge.
  • Prediction. D5 on rounds 0–1 gives ε̂. The population recursion with α′ = α + βε̂ then predicts the end entropy of each arm.
  • Pass. Both arms within 0.05 of their predictions, and the self-judging arm sharper than the frozen arm, as predicted.
  • Risk. A 0.5B judge may be close to random. Its bias may then be too small to matter, which would still be a result, but a weak one.

9Limits

  • Tuning. No test was preregistered. The D1 flag level 0.98 and the D1 floor were chosen with these runs in view. The D2 threshold is validated only by leaving one run out of nine, and the runs share panels, model and seed.
  • Probe panels. The probe panels were the training panels. Whether a held-out probe set shows the same α′ and ρ̂ depends on how training on some prompts moves others, and is untested.
  • D1 for IPO. D1 is accurate for the DPO runs, which solve each round nearly exactly. The IPO errors (up to 0.23) mean D1 measures the loop's actual contraction per round, which can differ from α′ when rounds are not solved.
  • D3 data. D3 rests on one judge and 130 panels with only three at-risk prompts, so its sensitivity of 1.0 rests on 3 prompts × 5 replicates. K = 4 throughout.
  • D4 scope. D4 counts directed cycles in hard labels. It does not convert the effect into a γB, so a positive test says that pooling creates cycles, not that the loop will rotate.
  • D5 judge model. D5 is tested only against a judge whose bias is linear in the policy log-ratio by construction. A real judge may be biased in other ways, which is X13's purpose.
  • Cost. All checks ran on CPU in minutes. No VM was started.
