Does the paper's story hold up, and what story should it tell JMLR?

2026-10-19. A reading of JMLR-B-v4 at 133d64c against the review, the literature audit and every neural batch so far, plus one new result checked numerically today. Code: reviews/2026-10-19-jmlr-storyline/analysis/. Every number below is in reviews/2026-10-19-jmlr-storyline/shared/sources.md.

Answer.The evidence holds up, but the story as written does not. The headline results are largely inherited from learning-in-games. The "cycling" half of the title rests on planted preferences, because natural feedback almost never cycles (1 of 130 jury panels lacks a Condorcet winner; 0 of 1,200 SPPO panels has a majority three-cycle). The most original theorem is in an appendix, framed as a cost. I propose one spine: the loop has two coordinates. The sharpness B decides where the loop goes. The refresh rule decides how fast it gets there. Every other pipeline knob either moves one of these two coordinates or acts through shared parameters, where the model stops applying. The second coordinate carries a result the paper has but does not state. At a fixed target, ordinary refresh needs about 2γ² rounds per e-fold, where γ is the cyclic gain. Anchoring to the second-latest policy instead needs about γ. On the planted panels this cuts the rounds to the target from 19 to 7 in the population model. One preregistered neural run of about an hour would test it.

Three problems follow from the table.

  • The title promises cycling, and the data say cycles are rare. On natural feedback, 1 of 130 jury panels lacks a Condorcet winner, and 0 of 1,200 SPPO panels contain a majority three-cycle. The stability theory is tested only on planted cycles. A referee will ask why the paper's second half matters.
  • The contributions are five parallel items with no spine. The new parts are scattered: the reduction in §3–4, the controls in §8.8, history in Appendix D, and T1 not in the paper at all. The inherited parts sit up front.
  • The failures are reported but not interpreted. E3's step budget, E6's weight averaging and X9's inner steps fail for the same reason: each acts through shared weights or optimizer state, which the population map does not contain. Read together, they mark the model's domain of validity, which is a result in its own right.

2The proposed spine: two coordinates

Thesis.Iterated preference optimization has two separable design coordinates. The target sharpness B decides where the loop goes, through the directional margins. The approach, meaning the refresh rule at that sharpness, decides how many rounds the loop needs to get there. That number is limited by the per-round step and by the cyclic gain γ. Practical knobs are changes of these coordinates, and their effects follow. The population model predicts a trained language model wherever a knob acts through an exactly solved round, and it fails where a knob acts through shared parameters or the optimizer.

Both halves now carry practical weight. On natural feedback, B is the main lever. A cyclic preference does not only make the loop cycle. It makes the loop slow, by a factor that the refresh rule controls, and that is where the paper's dynamics earn their place. Cycling becomes the edge case of slowness, so the title no longer leans on cycles being common.

3The new result: how fast the loop reaches its target

Fix the preference, the sampler and a target sharpness B, and let γB be the largest cyclic gain at the target. The comparison holds the target fixed and lets each scheme choose its refresh weight, with β = (1−α)B. The appendix theorem instead compares the schemes at the same α. That comparison shows the lag's cost and hides its gain.

Theorem (rounds to a fixed target; proposed). Let the step be capped at β ≤ β̄ ≤ B, and count rounds per e-fold of the local deviation.
  1. Ordinary refresh (reference πtαπref1−α). For every admissible α the spectral radius is at least max{1 − β̄/B, γB/√(1+γB2)}. So the round count is at least about max{B/β̄, 2γB2}.
  2. Maximal lagged reference (reference πt−1απref1−α, the same target). Every mode with gain at most 2√α decays at exactly √α. Choosing α = max{1 − β̄/B, s*2}, with s* = (√(1+γB2) − 1)/γB, gives about max{2B/β̄, γB} rounds.
  3. Hence, when rotation binds (β̄ ≥ 2B/γB), the lag reduces the round count by a factor tending to 2γB. When the step binds (β̄ ≤ B/(2γB2)), it costs a factor of 2, the "half speed" of the current Theorem 22. For C = 0 the step limit B/β̄ is attained exactly, without a log(1/ε) factor, by refreshing fully and then anchoring (T5).
Proof sketch. Part 1 combines Prop. 14 part 3 with the cap α ≥ 1 − β̄/B. Part 2 is Theorem 22 part 3 at ν = α, minimizing √α subject to (1−α)γB ≤ 2√α. Equality there is critical damping, and a slightly larger α avoids the repeated root. Part 3 compares the two. Every ingredient is already proved in the paper. What is new is the fixed-target optimization and the two-regime reading. ∎

Figure 1 checks the closed forms against a grid search over each scheme's parameters, including every lag ν ≤ α and extrapolation κ ≥ 0. The best lag is always the maximal one, and lagged sampling (optimism through the sampler) also scales linearly, at about 2γ. Figure 2 adds the step cap. The two-regime formula fits the exact optimum to within a few percent, and the crossing sits where the cap stops binding.

The local rates survive the nonlinear loop (Figure 3). The run uses the planted cycle panels at B = 12, from the trained model's own initial scores, with γB at a median of 1.96. The best ordinary refresh (α = 0.8) brings the median panel below KL 10−3 to stay at round 19, and the 90th percentile at round 24. Maximal lagged reference at α = 0.4 does it at rounds 7 and 9, and lagged sampling at rounds 9 and 12. The trained model under ordinary refresh tracks its population curve down to a noise floor of about KL 0.002. So the neural test should use the threshold 10−2. There the population predicts round 11 for ordinary refresh at α = 0.8 (the trained model took 17) and round 4 for the lag at α = 0.4.

What is inherited and what is ours

The separation itself is classical in games. Gradient descent–ascent needs a number of iterations quadratic in the condition number of a strongly monotone game. Extragradient, optimism and negative momentum need a linear number, and lower bounds show that linear is the best order recalled (Mokhtari, Ozdaglar and Pattathil 2020; Azizian et al. 2020; Ibrahim et al. 2020; Gidel et al. 2019). Our contribution is the mapping and its consequences:

  • anchoring plays the role of strong monotonicity and γB the condition number;
  • the momentum is a one-round lag in the KL reference, which every pipeline can implement by keeping one older checkpoint;
  • the step cap splits the comparison into two regimes;
  • a trained-model test is possible.

The paper already cites Gidel et al. and describes the lag as "negative momentum in logit space"; it has not drawn the conclusion.

  • Local rates. The nonlinear check covers one oracle family. The quadratic-versus-linear claim is asymptotic in γB, and the planted panels have γB ≈ 2.
  • Large steps. At B = 12 the lag uses β = 7.2 per round. v4 found the trained round less exact at large β (median residual 0.12 at β = 9.6). That is the main risk for the neural test.
  • Optimality of order γ within all one-evaluation-per-round refresh rules is my reading of the game lower bounds, not a proved statement here.

Title options, in order of preference: Where Iterated Preference Optimization Goes, and How Fast It Gets There; Target and Approach in Iterated Preference Optimization.

Abstract draft (about 200 words). Iterated preference optimization retrains a policy on comparisons of its own samples, often against a KL reference refreshed toward the latest policy. For population Ψ-preference optimization on a finite response set, we reduce this loop to an exact recursion with two separable coordinates. The target sharpness B = β/(1−α) decides where the loop goes. As in softmax learning with memory loss, the fixed point depends on the refresh weight α and the step β only through B, and directional margins set by the link, the labels and the sampler decide which response dominates and how far concentration goes. At a fixed target, the refresh rule decides how fast the loop gets there. A Hodge decomposition isolates the cyclic part of the preference as the only feedback channel. Its gain γ at the target makes ordinary refresh need about 2γ² rounds per e-fold, whereas anchoring to the second-latest policy needs about γ, the counterpart of the acceleration of optimistic and momentum methods in games. Judge bias, replay, entropy bonuses, drift budgets and DPO on non-Bradley–Terry labels act as explicit changes of these coordinates. Preregistered tests on a language model trained round by round confirm the predictions and locate where the population model stops: controls that act through shared parameters or the optimizer.

7Risks

  • X10 fails. If the trained round at β = 7.2 is too inexact, the lag may not show its speed-up. The theorem then stays as population theory and joins the boundary list, like X9. The α = 0.45 arm (β = 6.6) gives some margin.
  • "This is just GDA versus extragradient." We credit that literature in the theorem's statement. The contribution is the mapping, the two regimes under a step cap, the one-checkpoint implementation, and the test.
  • Natural feedback rarely produces large γ. Then the rotation limit rarely binds, and the step limit and B dominate in practice. The paper should say so plainly. Cyclic feedback arises from juries and heterogeneous annotators, and the identity link gives Bradley–Terry labels a small cyclic part too.
  • Length. Moving Appendix D's main result forward adds about a page; the cuts in §5 above remove more.
