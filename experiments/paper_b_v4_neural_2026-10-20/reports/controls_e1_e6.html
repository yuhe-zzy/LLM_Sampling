Do feedback controls stop collapse and oscillation in a real model?

2026-10-18. Neural tests E1–E6 of the controls proposed in the 2026-10-17 control report. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct on 64 short-answer panels; 16 training runs (a 15-run batch and one rerun); one H100 for about 5 hours, about $35. Code, predictions and runs: experiments/2026-10-17-controls/. Every number below is in reviews/2026-10-18-controls-neural/shared/sources.md.

Answer.Besides the paper's three knobs (refresh weight α, step β, on-policy share λ), the iterated preference loop can be controlled through what the judge rewards, what the reference is anchored to, how much memory the sampler has, how α is scheduled, and which model is deployed, and our 2026-10-17 theory predicts what each does to collapse and oscillation. Trained round by round, a real language model behaved as frozen predictions said in four of six tests: a judge that favours the current model's likely answers acts as hidden refresh and collapses the policy past the predicted threshold, an anchor keeps the model closer to its start than an entropy bonus at equal entropy, replay of earlier rounds removes oscillation under fast refresh and creates it under slow refresh, and raising α on a schedule removes oscillation without moving the target. Two controls failed as specified: a drift budget enforced by shrinking the step let 16 of 64 panels overshoot, because shared weights keep moving a panel whose step is zero (a "refresh, then anchor" version, fixed before a rerun, put all 64 panels on target), and averaging network weights did not reproduce the averaged policy, although serving a random recent checkpoint does. Uncertain: one seed per setting, short fixed answers, oscillation only on planted cycles, and a speed advantage of the rerun controller found after the fact.
Status. None of these results is in the manuscript. Section 8 lists what could enter v4 and in what form; nothing will be edited until you approve.

1Question and setting

Iterated preference optimization trains each model generation on comparisons of the previous generation's answers. The v4 paper shows two ways this loop goes wrong. With full refresh of the reference, or with hard labels, the policy collapses: one answer takes all the probability. With a cycle in the preferences and fast refresh, the policy oscillates: successive generations rotate among answers instead of settling. The 2026-10-17 report collected evidence that both patterns do harm in deployed models (homogenized answers and lost diversity for collapse; release-to-release swings such as the 2025 sycophancy reversals for oscillation) and proposed controls beyond α, β and λ. This report tests those controls on a real model.

A control here is a rule that changes one part of a training round, sometimes in response to the state of the policy. Figure 1 shows the round and the five places where the tested controls act.

4Design and its logic

4.1 Testbed

The testbed is the one of the v4 validation. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct is fully fine-tuned in fp32. A panel is a prompt with four short answers fixed in advance, and the policy on a panel is the softmax of the model's sequence log-probabilities of the four answers. One outer round sets the reference and the sampler from the current model, minimizes the expected-label loss for 40 Adam steps at learning rate from a fresh optimizer, and rescores the answers. All runs use the 64-panel subset of v4 and expected labels, so there is no label-sampling noise.

Two kinds of labels serve two purposes:

  • Reward-model labels (E1–E3): Skywork-Reward-Llama-3.1-8B-v0.2 scores turned into soft Bradley–Terry probabilities at temperature 3.168, trained with DPO (logit link) at λ = 0.8 for 60 rounds. These labels order the answers, so they test the collapse controls.
  • Planted cycles (E4–E6): preference matrices we set so that 32 balanced panels contain a three-answer majority cycle and 32 decisive panels have a clear winner, trained with IPO (identity link) at λ = 1 and B = 12. These test the oscillation controls; natural reward-model panels almost never cycle (v4 report, Section 7).

4.3 Measures

  • Entropy: mean over panels of the policy's entropy, in nats, at the last round. Four equally likely answers give 1.39.
  • KL to the initial policy (mean over panels) measures how far training moved the model from where it started.
  • KL to a target: per panel, summarized by the median; the target is the exact population fixed point at the stated B.
  • Drift of a panel: .
  • Oscillating share: the fraction of the 32 balanced panels whose probability amplitude over the last 30 rounds, after removing a linear trend, exceeds 0.0536 (the v4 rule).
  • Top-two gap: the median log-odds gap between a panel's two most likely answers.

5.1 Training loss

The runs logged the loss at the first and the last of the 40 inner steps of every round; the steps in between were not logged. Recomputing the step-1 loss from the saved panel scores reproduces the logged value to a relative error of or better in 15 of the 16 runs. In the step-budget run the error reaches 3.2%, in rounds whose smallest active β is about : the DPO loss divides the score gap by β, which magnifies float32 rounding. The recomputation also gives each round's exact optimum. Each round has its own objective, so the loss need not fall across rounds; what matters is how far above its own optimum each round ends.

Under full refresh each round starts with the trained model equal to its reference, so the loss starts at log 2 and 40 steps bring it to the round's optimum. The step-budget run behaves differently: as panels use up their budget their β falls toward zero, and the logged step-40 loss reaches 3,296, with 52 of 60 rounds ending above 10. Its rounds end a median of 102 above their optimum (10th–90th percentile 13–1,200). Every other reward-model run ends its rounds 0.00002–0.003 above, and the planted-cycle runs 0.002–0.010 above, which is the noise floor of a freshly started Adam optimizer seen in v4 (Figure 3).

5.3 E1: a judge biased toward the current model is hidden refresh

At α = 0.5 and β = 1, Proposition 1 says a bias acts like refresh , so the loop has no target from on. The end entropies follow the predicted targets: 0.638, 0.300 and 0.061 at 0, 0.3 and 0.45, against 0.638, 0.301 and 0.066. At the policy collapses (entropy 0.007) and the top-two gap keeps growing, from 29.1 to 38.6 nats between rounds 30 and 60. The trained model's gap grows far more slowly than the population gap (44 to 235) ours: the logistic loss is nearly flat at large margins, so 40 steps barely widen a gap that is already large, whereas the population model jumps to each round's exact optimum. The criterion (growth of at least 1.2×) passes. The same bias computed from the initial model's scores, as a frozen judge would apply it, leaves the entropy at 0.525 (predicted 0.527), far from collapse.

Why it matters. A loop whose judge shares the policy, or rewards what the current model already finds likely, spends part of its stability budget without anyone setting α ours. A frozen judge, a judge from another model family, or regressing the policy's log-probability gap out of the labels removes that feedback.

5.4 E2: an entropy bonus prevents collapse but forgets the base model

Under full refresh, a popularity penalty is the only brake, and it does stop collapse: entropies 0.186 and 0.416. Partial refresh at B = 8 and B = 4 reaches about the same entropies (0.195 and 0.404). At these matched entropies the bonus runs end farther from the initial model, KL 1.60 against 1.26 and 1.38 against 0.93, as predicted (1.61 / 1.25 and 1.37 / 0.94).

An entropy bonus spreads probability toward what the reward ranks well; an anchor keeps the base model's own distribution ours. If diversity is the goal because the base model's spread carries information (calibration, minority answers), the anchor is the control that keeps it.

5.5 E3 and E3′: budget the drift with a restoring force

The per-round KL controller collapses the policy as Proposition 4 predicts: entropy at round 60 after a cumulative β of 394 (predicted 401). Holding each round's step small does not bound where the loop ends.

The step budget failed. Its median panel ended at the target (KL 0.0083), but the mean entropy fell to 0.095 against the target's 0.196, and 16 of 64 panels overshot (KL to the target above 0.1). Two facts in the logs explain it:

  • No restoring force. A panel whose budget is used gets β = 0 and leaves the loss, but it shares the network with the other panels and keeps moving. In the last 10 rounds 77% of panel-rounds had β = 0, and nothing pulled an overshooting panel back.
  • Exploding losses. With β near zero, the DPO loss of nearly exhausted panels explodes (Section 5.1) and its gradients push the shared weights.

The book prompt shows the effect (Figure 4): the policy ends on an answer the target barely supports. In the population model, where panels do not share parameters, the same controller stops at the target (median KL ), so this is a failure of the step-only design in a shared network, not of the budget idea.

The rerun. Before the rerun we specified a controller with a restoring force: full refresh at β = 1 until a panel's drift reaches its budget, then partial refresh at α = 0.875, whose target is the same B = 8 point and which pulls the policy back toward it. Its frozen prediction was median KL ≈ 0, entropy 0.196, no panel above 0.1 and a switch at round 9. The trained model matched it: median KL (90th percentile 0.0011, maximum 0.0044), entropy 0.197, no panel above 0.1, switch at round 9 (range 6–13), and no loss blow-up (largest step-40 loss 0.52).

Speed post hoc. The rerun ends where the plain B = 8 anchor ends (KL to the initial policy 1.26 in both runs), but gets there about twice as fast: its median KL to the target first falls below 0.001 at round 8, against round 16 for anchoring from the first round (population 8 against 18). This comparison was not preregistered.

5.6 E4: replay cuts both ways

Replay with memory (each round's sampler keeps 80% of the previous sampler) leaves the B = 12 targets unchanged but changes their stability, in opposite directions at the two refresh weights. At α = 0.5 the oscillating share of balanced panels falls from 0.94 without replay (v4) to 0.00. At α = 0.8, where no panel oscillated without replay, it rises to 0.59. Predictions: 0.00 and 0.53. The α = 0.8 runs sit near the threshold (median amplitude 0.066, against 0.056 predicted and a cut-off of 0.0536), and one decisive panel of 32 (0.03) also crossed it.

The no-replay runs come from v4 and ran 80 rounds; the replay runs ran 150. All shares are measured over each run's last 30 rounds.

Why it matters. Mixing samples from earlier models is common in iterative preference learning and looks like a neutral variance reduction. In this loop it is a stability knob whose sign depends on the refresh weight ours.

5.7 E5: adaptive refresh removes oscillation without moving the target

Starting at α = 0.2, where the planted panels oscillate, the model-free rule raised α along the predicted path (0.2, 0.44, 0.61, 0.73 over the first 100 rounds) and kept β = (1−α) 12, so the target stayed fixed. It then kept going to 0.91: near the target the optimizer's noise floor keeps the mean step from falling by 20%, so the rule keeps firing, which the preregistration allowed. No balanced panel oscillates at the end, and the median KL to the target is 0.0009. The stability threshold at the median balanced panel is α = 0.586.

5.8 E6: deploy the average as a mixture, not as averaged weights

The α = 0.2 run oscillates: its last iterate is a median KL 0.479 from the target on balanced panels. The arithmetic average of the last 30 policies is 0.0080 away, close to v4's 0.0077. Averaging the weights of those 30 checkpoints gives a model 0.052 away, 6.5 times the policy average, so E6 fails. The weight-averaged model is not even the geometric (log-probability) average, which is 0.016 away; the two differ by KL 0.031. In a nonlinear network the average of the weights is neither average of the policies.

A deployable version. Serving a uniformly random checkpoint from the last 30 rounds for each request makes the distribution of answers exactly the arithmetic average ours. It costs 30 stored checkpoints, and a user may meet different checkpoints within one conversation. Distilling the mixture into one model is the untested alternative.

6Conclusions and judgment

1

What the judge rewards is part of the refresh budget.

A bias toward the current model's likely answers adds to the refresh weight: the trained model followed the predicted targets to within 0.005 nats of entropy and collapsed past , while a frozen judge with the same bias did not.

2

Bound the drift with a restoring force, not with the step.

Per-round KL control collapses; a step budget overshoots in a shared network; an entropy bonus stops collapse but forgets the base model. Switching to an anchor once the drift budget is used put every panel on the target, about twice as fast as anchoring from the start.

3

Sampler memory and the refresh schedule are stability knobs.

Replay stabilizes fast refresh and destabilizes slow refresh at the same target, both as predicted; raising α with β = (1−α)B removes oscillation without changing where the loop ends.

4

Deploy the average as a mixture.

An oscillating loop's time average is near its target, but weight averaging does not deliver it; serving a random recent checkpoint does.

5

What the experiment does not show.

Seed-to-seed variation; sampled rather than expected labels; long-form answers, parameter-efficient fine-tuning or larger models; oscillation under natural feedback (E4–E6 use planted cycles); combinations of controls; and Proposition 3 on pair selection.

Verdict by the preregistered rule. E1, E2, E4 and E5 pass as frozen. E3 fails as frozen: its per-round half passes and its step-budget half fails. E3′ passes under a condition changed after that failure; the change, its prediction and an unchanged criterion were logged before the rerun. E6 is refuted and reported without a rerun. The speed advantage of E3′ is a post hoc observation.

Confidence. High for E1 and E2: the trained runs match the frozen numbers to within 0.005 in entropy and 0.01 in KL. High for the direction of E4 and E5, with the α = 0.8 replay case close to the oscillation threshold. Moderate for E3′: one run, designed after seeing the failure, although its prediction was frozen first. E6 is a clear negative for weight averaging at this scale.

7What changes in the 2026-10-17 control report

  • Section 5.4 said a budget on KL to the initial policy stops at the same target as partial refresh. That holds in the population model, not in a network with a step controller. The practical rule becomes: budget the drift, and enforce the budget by switching to an anchor or another restoring force, never by shrinking the step.
  • Section 5.6 named weight averaging as the practical proxy for deploying the average. That is refuted here; the proxy is per-request sampling of recent checkpoints, or distillation of the mixture (untested).
  • Propositions 1, 2, 4 (its per-round half) and 5, and the adaptive-refresh rule, are now confirmed on a trained model in the regime tested.

Limits.

  • One seed per setting. Agreement across 64 panels is strong, but panels share one network, so they are not independent replicates.
  • Expected labels only: no label-sampling noise, which v4 showed amplifies with the step.
  • Fixed four-answer panels of short answers; full fine-tuning of a 0.5B model at one learning rate.
  • The oscillation controls (E4–E6) use planted cycles; the collapse controls (E1–E3) use one reward model.
  • The no-replay comparison runs of E4 come from v4 and are shorter (80 rounds against 150).
  • E3′ was designed after the E3 failure; its speed comparison was not preregistered.
  • Only the losses at inner steps 1 and 40 were logged.
  • Cost and timing are approximate.
