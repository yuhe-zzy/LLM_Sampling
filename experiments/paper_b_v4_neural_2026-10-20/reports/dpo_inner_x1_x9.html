1Question and setting

Iterated preference optimization trains each new model on comparisons of the previous model's answers, against a KL reference that is partly refreshed toward the latest model. The paper reduces one round to a population map on the log-probabilities of a fixed set of answers, then analyses where the loop goes and whether it gets there. The JMLR review raised two gaps between that map and how people actually train.

  • DPO is modelled through the logit link. With the logit link, a population DPO round is exactly the paper's round when the labels are Bradley–Terry, that is, generated by one score per answer. Labels with a majority cycle are not Bradley–Terry. Our theorem T1 shows that on such labels the DPO round fits a weighted Bradley–Terry model to them, which is a different map. On the panels used here the two maps disagree about whether the loop oscillates.
  • Each round is assumed to be solved exactly. Pipelines take a fixed number of optimizer steps per round. Our theorem T6 shows that with gradient descent a partial solve moves only part of the way to the round's optimum, which is a slower refresh at the same target. That would make the number of inner steps a stability knob.

X1 asks which map actual DPO follows on intransitive labels. X9 asks whether fewer inner steps stabilize a loop that oscillates when each round is solved.

4Design and its logic

4.1 Testbed

The setup is the testbed of the v4 validation and the controls batch, unchanged. Qwen2.5-0.5B-Instruct is fully fine-tuned in fp32 on 64 fixed panels of four short answers each. The labels come from a planted oracle. On every panel three answers form a rock–paper–scissors cycle of strength 0.3 and the fourth loses to all. On the 32 balanced panels the cycle is a majority cycle, with no Condorcet winner. On the 32 decisive panels one answer is a Condorcet winner. Labels are expected, not sampled. Each round starts a fresh Adam optimizer at learning rate 2e-5.

  • Oscillating. Over the last 30 rounds, remove a linear trend from each answer's probability. A panel oscillates when the largest remaining standard deviation exceeds 0.0536, four times the noise floor of the converged v4 runs (the v4 rule).
  • Fraction of the exact move f. In centred log-probabilities, , where F is the exact round applied to the model's own state. f = 1 means the round was solved.
  • Moving. The mean over balanced panels and the last 30 rounds of the largest change in any answer's probability from one round to the next. A run counts as stopped when this is below 0.01.
  • Distance to the target. KL(time-averaged policy ‖ target), with the median over balanced panels.

5.1 Training loss

Each outer round is a new objective, because the reference and the sampler move with the model, so the loss does not fall across rounds. Figure 1 shows each round from the loss before its inner steps to the loss after them, against the exact optimum of that round, recomputed from the saved scores. The logged losses match the recomputed ones to a relative 5e-7. With 40 steps, the DPO rounds of X1 end a median 0.00066 and 0.00020 above their optimum (maximum 0.0092), so they are solved. With fewer steps they are not. The median excess after the steps is 0.040 at k = 10 and 0.39 at k = 3. At k = 1 it is 3.77, higher than the 1.05 before the step: a single step overshoots.

5.2 X1: actual DPO follows the weighted Bradley–Terry map

Figure 2 gives the preregistered test. At B = 4, α = 0.8, 3 of 32 balanced panels oscillate (0.094); T1 predicts none, the logit map 25. At B = 2, α = 0.3, 10 oscillate (0.313); T1 predicts 9, the logit map 24. Decisive panels oscillate on 0 and 3 of 32. Both runs fall inside the pass band, and the logit map is 0.69 and 0.44 away. X1 passes.

The agreement holds panel by panel (Figure 3, post hoc). The trained model gives the same verdict as T1 on 91% of balanced panels in both runs, against 31% and 56% for the logit map. Applied to the model's own state each round, the T1 map is closer to the next state than the logit map: median one-step error 0.080 and 0.074 against 0.125 and 0.190. Most trained amplitudes sit at 0.02–0.05, the optimizer's noise level, where T1 predicts almost nothing. One panel at B = 4 (Python frameworks) oscillates in the trained model (amplitude 0.22) although T1 gives 0.006.

5.3 X1 on single prompts

Figures 4 and 5 follow two balanced panels through both settings. On the golden-retriever prompt at B = 4, the trained model and T1's map spiral in together to a mix of the three cycle answers, while the logit map keeps rotating. At B = 2 all three rotate, and the trained model stays close to T1's oscillation for 120 rounds. On the instrument prompt the picture is the same: amplitude 0.011 against T1's 0.010 and the logit map's 0.155 at B = 4, and 0.139 against T1's 0.140 at B = 2.

5.4 X9: fewer inner steps do not stabilize the cycle

Figure 6 gives the preregistered test. Balanced panels oscillate at 30, 28, 32 and 30 of 32 for k = 40, 10, 3 and 1. The share is not monotone and never comes near 0.2, so the ordinal prediction is refuted. Decisive panels, which converge with solved rounds, start to oscillate as k falls: 1, 4, 12 and 20 of 32. The calibrated prediction was right at k = 40 and k = 1, but there it predicted no stabilization. At k = 10 and k = 3, where it predicted stabilization (0.26 and 0.00), it was wrong.

No run stopped moving (mean step 0.21, 0.20, 0.27 and 0.28 against the 0.01 threshold), so the target check is not testable as specified. Post hoc, the policy averaged over the last 30 rounds still sits on the B = 12 target. Its median KL to the target over balanced panels is 0.0003, 0.0007, 0.0031 and 0.0064 at k = 40, 10, 3 and 1. The oscillation stays centred on the same point.

5.5 Why: a few fresh-Adam steps take a step of roughly fixed size post hoc

T6 needs the inner solver to move a fixed fraction of the way to the round's optimum, whatever the distance. Figure 7(a) measures that fraction, binned by how far the exact round would move.

  • k = 40 and k = 10. At k = 40 the fraction is 1.00 in every quartile, so the rounds are exact. At k = 10 it stays between 0.91 and 1.03.
  • k = 3 and k = 1. The fraction is about 1.1 when the exact move is small and falls to 0.06–0.08 when it is large. The achieved move barely depends on the exact move: the log-log slope of one on the other is 0.27 and 0.38, against 0.98 for exact rounds.

This is how a normalized optimizer behaves. Adam's first steps from a fresh state move each parameter by about the learning rate, whatever the size of the gradient, so the change in the panel scores does not shrink as the state nears the round's optimum. Near the target the round overshoots, which acts like a faster refresh, not a slower one. That keeps every panel moving, including decisive ones. Figure 7(b) shows the overshoot in the loss: one step raises the median excess over the optimum from 1.05 to 3.77.

5.6 X9 on single prompts

Figure 8 follows a decisive panel across the four step counts. With solved rounds it settles on its target within a few rounds (amplitude 0.016). With fewer steps it becomes noisier, then it jumps between the winner and the runner-up: amplitude 0.036, 0.101 and 0.175. Figure 9 shows a balanced panel, which rotates at every step count (0.217, 0.210, 0.267 and 0.286).

6Conclusions and judgment

1

On intransitive labels, DPO is its own map, and T1 predicts it.

The paper's logit-link model of DPO is exact only for Bradley–Terry labels. On majority cycles the trained model follows T1's weighted Bradley–Terry round: the preregistered shares match within 0.1, the per-panel verdict agrees on 91% of panels, and the one-step error is smaller than the logit map's. On these panels actual DPO is much more stable than the logit model says (0% against 78% predicted to oscillate at B = 4). Treating DPO through the logit link would overstate its cycling on intransitive data.

2

Cutting inner Adam steps is not a stability knob.

T6 holds for gradient descent, but a freshly started Adam optimizer takes steps of roughly fixed size. A few steps overshoot the round's optimum near the target and undershoot it far away. The loop does not move toward slower refresh. It keeps rotating, and panels that converge with solved rounds start to jump. In practice the refresh weight, not the step count, is what stabilizes the loop; a short inner loop adds oscillation of its own.

3

The target survives an inexact inner solver.

Even where every panel oscillates, the policy averaged over the last 30 rounds lies on the same B = 12 target (median KL ≤ 0.0064). Inexact rounds change how the loop moves around the target, not where the target is. This matches the paper's fixed-point statements and E6's finding that a mixture of recent checkpoints serves the target.

What the experiment does not show.

  • T6 itself is not tested: the inner solver was Adam, not gradient descent. A run with plain SGD inner steps would test it.
  • X1 covers two (B,α) cells chosen where the maps disagree most, on one oracle with cycle strength 0.3. It does not show how actual DPO behaves on natural intransitive feedback.
  • The Adam explanation in Section 5.5 is post hoc. It fits the step sizes and the loss, but it was not predicted.

Limits.

  • One seed per setting. The 64 panels share one network, so they are not independent replicates.
  • Expected labels only; one learning rate (2e-5); full fine-tuning of a 0.5B model on short fixed panels.
  • The k = 40 reference is the v4 run (80 rounds), not a new 150-round run.
  • The oscillation threshold 0.0536 was calibrated on converged IPO runs; X1 trained amplitudes cluster at 0.02–0.05, close to it.
